## Definition
The measurable accumulation of cognitive disengagement that results from habitually offloading thinking to AI tools. Coined by Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab, 2025) to describe the finding that repeated LLM-assisted writing reduced brain connectivity, memory retention, and sense of authorship in a controlled longitudinal study — and that the deficit grew across sessions.

Not the same as "lower cognitive load" (which LLMs do achieve). Cognitive debt is the cost you pay later: degraded ability to think through the problem independently, weakened recall of your own work, and eroded agency.

## Why it matters
The marketing claim for AI tools is that they reduce cognitive load, which sounds good. But cognitive load theory distinguishes three types: extraneous load (bad — irrelevant mental effort), intrinsic load (neutral — inherent task complexity), and germane load (good — effort that builds schemas and long-term understanding). LLMs primarily reduce germane load. That's the load you actually want.

The practical implication: using ChatGPT to draft something may produce a better-looking artifact while leaving you worse at the underlying skill. And this compounds. Session 1 harm is modest. By sessions 2 and 3, the EEG connectivity gap between LLM and Brain-only groups widens.

## Key findings from the study
- **Memory:** LLM group, 0/18 produced a correct verbatim quote from their own essay minutes after writing it. Search Engine and Brain-only groups: ~88% success rate. This is one of the starkest single statistics in the paper.
- **Ownership:** Only the LLM group reported zero ownership. Brain-only: 89% full ownership.
- **EEG:** Neural coupling scaled inversely with external support: LLM < Search Engine < Brain-only across all four frequency bands.
- **Crossover:** Participants switched tools in session 4 without warning. Prior LLM users showed weaker neural engagement even when forced to work brain-only. Prior brain-only users showed higher memory recall when given LLM access. The habit shapes the brain's response in subsequent sessions.
- **Satisfaction paradox:** LLM group self-reported ~94% satisfaction despite the worst recall and lowest ownership. They felt good about work they couldn't remember.

## Caveats
- Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.
- Lab setting, 20-minute SAT-style essays, all top-Boston-university students — narrow and somewhat artificial.
- n=54 (18 per group).
- The study used ChatGPT specifically; results may not generalize to all LLM usage patterns (e.g., Socratic dialogue vs. answer-generation).
- The effect is specifically on memory/learning outcomes, not necessarily on the quality of the artifact produced.

## The competence paradox
Higher-competence learners use LLMs strategically — to reduce extraneous strain while remaining deeply engaged with the material. Lower-competence learners offload entirely, which accelerates the debt accumulation. This means LLMs may be most cognitively harmful to exactly the people who already struggle — amplifying the gap rather than closing it.

## Related concepts
- **Cognitive offloading** (established): delegating cognitive tasks to external tools or environments. Not inherently bad — notes, calculators, calendars are all cognitive offloading. The debt accrues specifically when the offloaded process is one you need to build.
- **Google Effect** (transactive memory): people remember where to find information rather than the information itself. LLMs create an even stronger version — you don't even remember the information was found.
- **Metacognitive laziness**: offloading not just the object-level task but the self-regulation and reflection around it.

## Open questions
- Does the effect generalize to uses beyond essay writing? (Coding, analysis, decision-making?)
- Is there a safe mode — using LLMs as Socratic interlocutors rather than answer-generators — that avoids the debt?
- Can the debt be repaid? Session 4 suggests residual effects persist even when tools are removed.
- Where's the threshold? Occasional use vs. habitual use presumably produce different outcomes.
